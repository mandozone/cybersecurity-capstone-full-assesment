Engagement Overview

An authorized, non-destructive penetration-test of the Artemis lab — observation first, exploitation never.

The engagement was scoped against a single Windows host inside an isolated VirtualBox lab network (attacker 10.10.10.5 Kali Linux, target 10.10.10.25). The methodology follows the structure of a real security-operations workflow: reconnaissance, target identification, vulnerability identification, threat assessment, and executive plus technical reporting.

The deliverable is not a list of CVEs — it is a defensible chain of evidence showing how an unauthenticated network observer can move from public OSINT to a CVSS-scored finding, and what a remediation owner needs to do about it. Active exploitation was deliberately out of scope; every finding was validated against at least two independent tools.

Methodology

Five phases, each gated on the artefacts of the last.

The five phases are run in sequence because each one narrows the next. Reconnaissance defines the universe of possible targets; scanning narrows it to live services; vulnerability identification narrows it to weak ones; threat assessment narrows it to the ones that actually matter to the business; and reporting turns the chain of evidence into an action plan.

P1

Reconnaissance

Passive OSINT — social, infrastructure, and lab-only phishing design. No packets to target.

 P2

Target Identification & Scanning

Confirm host reachability, enumerate exposed services, map the attack surface.

 P3

Vulnerability Identification

Run a vulnerability scanner, validate findings with a second tool, document evidence.

 P4

Threat Assessment

Likelihood × impact across deployment contexts, defensive controls, and CVSS scoring.

 P5

Reporting

Executive summary + detailed technical report — one engagement, two audiences.

Phase 1 · Reconnaissance

Passive OSINT only — zero packets sent to the target.

The scope of Phase 1 was strictly passive: no scanning, no exploitation, no contact with personnel. The goal was to demonstrate how much attack-relevant information an outsider can assemble from public sources alone, and to narrow the attack surface before any active phase begins.

Social & Human Recon

  • Reddit: Public complaint about an SSH outage from a Times Square payroll manager
  • LinkedIn: ~12 IT and security staff enumerated from public profiles
  • Twitter / Instagram: General lifestyle patterns observed (no PII captured)
  • BeenVerified: Conceptual e-mail verification noted; no lookup executed

Infrastructure & Domain Recon

  • theHarvester: 43 emails, 17 subdomains
  • crt.sh: 22 additional subdomains via Certificate Transparency
  • DNSDumpster: DNS map produced; on-prem MX server identified
  • Shodan: 2 public IPs exposed; Apache 2.4.41 fingerprinted
  • Wayback Machine: Legacy /admin path located
  • Google Dorks: 3 internal PDF documents surfaced
  • Rapid7 Job Board: Kubernetes + Duo MFA rollout confirmed
  • Glassdoor: References to legacy mainframe systems
  • GrayHatWarfare: No exposed public S3 buckets identified
  • Nmap (ping-only): 3 live edge hosts detected — no service scan
  • Proxychains + Tor: Passive browsing routed for source privacy

Phishing Design (Lab-Only)

  • SET (Social Engineering Toolkit): Starbucks login portal cloned inside local VM
  • Gophish: Phishing campaign template authored — no SMTP configured, no campaigns sent

Designed and staged for documentation; no email was ever sent and no live target ever interacted with the template.

  • 0 packets sent to any in-scope or out-of-scope production system
  • Attack surface mapped and narrowed for transition into Phase 2

Phase 2 · Target Identification & Scanning

From a public footprint to a single, confirmed live host.

Phase 2 closed the gap between OSINT and a defined attack surface. A single Windows host was confirmed reachable inside the lab network, RDP was identified as the exposed service, and the operating-system fingerprint plus SSL/TLS metadata were captured as inputs to Phase 3.

Masscan — rapid exposure

Used as a wide-net first-pass to confirm reachability and identify the high-value exposed port. Trades depth for speed.

$ sudo masscan 10.10.10.25 -p3389 --rate 1000 -v
Starting masscan 1.3.2
Scanning 1 hosts [1 port/host]
Discovered open port 3389/tcp on 10.10.10.25

Nmap — service & OS fingerprint

Followed Masscan to add service version, default-script context, and OS detection on the single open port.

Phase 3 · Vulnerability Identification

Identify, validate, document — every finding confirmed by a second tool.

Phase 3 ran a vulnerability scanner against the in-scope host, then validated each finding with at least one independent tool. The deliverable is not a Nessus PDF — it is a chain of evidence, with screenshots, that survives review by a sceptical defender.

Tool 1

Masscan — Rapid Exposure Identification

Re-run against the Phase 3 host (192.168.56.104) to anchor the chain of custody and re-confirm exposed services before deeper analysis.

sudo masscan 192.168.56.104 -p3389 --rate 1000 -v
Pros: Extremely fast; low overhead; ideal for rapid surface enumeration.
Cons: No service version detection; no vulnerability identification.
Tool 2

Nmap — Service & OS Enumeration

Added service version, default-script context, and OS fingerprint. Confirmed Microsoft RDP with attached SSL certificate metadata.

sudo nmap -sV -sC -O 192.168.56.104
Pros: Detailed service & OS identification; script engine adds security context.
Cons: Slower than Masscan; OS fingerprinting can return low-confidence matches.
Tool 3

Nessus — Vulnerability Identification & Risk Scoring

A Nessus Essentials scan flagged a medium-severity finding: SSL Certificate Cannot Be Trusted — self-signed certificate, broken trust chain, identical issuer and subject. The first finding in this engagement to carry CVSS-style scoring.

FINDING · MEDIUM SSL Certificate Cannot Be Trusted
  • Self-signed X.509 certificate presented by RDP
  • No trusted certificate chain
  • Issuer == Subject (no third-party validation)
  • Facilitates man-in-the-middle attacks if reachable from untrusted networks
Pros: CVSS-based severity scoring; clear remediation; industry-recognised engine.
Cons: Limited without authenticated credentials; can generate false positives; requires analyst interpretation.
Tool 4

Metasploit — Validation (Non-Exploitative)

An auxiliary SSL scanner module was executed to independently confirm SSL/TLS behaviour. No exploit was run — Metasploit was used purely as a second opinion against the Nessus finding.

msf6 > use auxiliary/scanner/ssl/openssl_heartbeat
msf6 auxiliary(openssl_heartbeat) > set RHOSTS 192.168.56.104
msf6 auxiliary(openssl_heartbeat) > run
Pros: Validates scanner findings; protocol-level insight; non-exploitative at this stage.
Cons: Requires correct module choice; no automatic risk score; not a replacement for a vulnerability scanner.
Tool 5

Burp Suite — Application-Layer Traffic Analysis

Burp was configured as an intercepting proxy between the browser and the target host. Used to demonstrate visibility into application-layer traffic and capture HTTP request/response pairs against the same host that exposed RDP.

Pros: Deep visibility into application traffic; essential for web-layer testing.
Cons: Manual configuration; community edition has limited automation.

Phase 4 · Threat Assessment

From a Nessus finding to a defensible business risk.

Phase 4 took the confirmed cryptographic weakness from Phase 3 and reasoned about it as a business risk: which CIA properties it breaks, what an attacker could realistically chain it into, how likely that is across deployment contexts, and what a remediation owner can do about it. Output: a CVSS v3.1 base score and a likelihood / impact matrix.

Primary Confirmed Vulnerability

Untrusted (self-signed) SSL/TLS certificate on remote-access service

Combined with legacy cryptographic configuration on the RDP listener, this weakens three foundational properties:

  • Confidentiality — encrypted traffic cannot be reliably trusted
  • Authenticity — the identity of the remote endpoint cannot be verified
  • Integrity — encrypted sessions may be subject to interception or manipulation

Acceptable in isolation in a lab; meaningfully risky in any production or remote-access scenario.

Realistic Threat Scenario (Production Context)

  1. Service discovery — attacker locates the exposed remote-access service through indexing or scanning.
  2. Endpoint impersonation — because the certificate is untrusted, the attacker can stand up a look-alike endpoint without triggering reliable trust validation.
  3. Traffic interception — interception occurs over a compromised path (open Wi-Fi, rogue gateway, internal hop).
  4. User trust bypass — the user clicks past the certificate warning, as they have been conditioned to.
  5. Credential exposure — authentication material or session data is captured; lateral movement becomes possible.

Technical Impact

  • Loss of secure authentication assurance
  • Increased risk of credential interception
  • Session compromise potential

Operational Impact

  • Unauthorized system access
  • Lateral movement across internal systems
  • Service availability concerns

Business Impact

  • Increased incident response and recovery costs
  • Compliance and audit exposure
  • Loss of trust in remote-access infrastructure

Defensive Controls & Remediation

Blocking mechanisms

  • Endpoint security software (AV / EDR)
  • Network-based intrusion detection (IDS / IPS)
  • Certificate-validation enforcement at the client
  • Multi-factor authentication on the remote-access service

Recommended remediation

  • Replace the self-signed certificate with a CA-issued certificate
  • Enforce certificate validation for remote services
  • Restrict remote access to trusted networks and users
  • Implement multi-factor authentication
  • Monitor and log all remote-access activity

Phase 5 · Reporting

One engagement, two audiences — management and remediation owners.

The final deliverable is split into an Executive Summary (a non-technical management report with a risk matrix and four executive recommendations) and a Detailed Technical Report (scope, objectives, assumptions, timeline, reconnaissance activities, identified vulnerabilities, threat analysis, recommendations, conclusion). The split is intentional: a CISO does not need ssl-cert: Self-signed, and a systems administrator does not need a quarterly board narrative.

Risk AreaDescriptionLikelihoodBusiness ImpactOverall Risk
Remote Access Exposure RDP services accessible beyond trusted network boundaries. Medium High Medium
Cryptographic Weakness Self-signed SSL certificates reduce trust assurance and increase exposure to interception and impersonation. Medium Medium Medium
Lateral Movement Potential Initial compromise could allow attackers to expand control across internal systems and services. Low–Medium High Medium

Executive Recommendations

  • Replace self-signed certificates with CA-issued certificates to restore trust and protect data.
  • Limit remote access to approved users and locations through improved network controls.
  • Require additional identity verification (MFA) for administrative access to critical systems.
  • Perform regular system reviews and security assessments as the environment evolves.

Key takeaway — the discipline is in what we deliberately did not do.

The hardest part of this engagement was not finding a vulnerability — it was demonstrating that the vulnerability was real without firing a single exploit. Two independent tools confirmed the same SSL/TLS weakness. A CVSS v3.1 score and a likelihood / impact matrix translated it into a number a manager could act on. A two-audience report made sure both the boardroom and the workstation got the answer they actually needed.
